# Access needs and reusable scripts

A personal collection of what helps, where it helps and words you can reuse to ask for it. This guide covers Jot's existing `access-needs-and-scripts@1` profile, not a tested clinical intervention. Relevant source sections were checked on 2026-11-02; the review scope is in section 28.

> No diagnosis is needed to keep this collection. A request can describe a need without including a life history. Formal accommodation processes vary by setting and jurisdiction; this guide does not determine eligibility or guarantee that a request will be accepted.

## 2. Summary

- Keep one need per entry, in your own language.
- Add what helps, where it applies and an optional phrase to copy or show.
- Reuse wording instead of explaining everything again.
- Keep the full collection private; share only the extract you choose.
- Change or remove a request when it no longer fits. Nothing needs daily monitoring.

## 3. History and origin

Written requests, access cards and communication supports have many origins. This guide does not claim that Jot invented them.

| Date | Relevant source or local artifact |

The sources explain different settings and responsibilities. Their inclusion is not evidence that a personal script collection has been evaluated.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **The need comes first.** You do not have to write a persuasive essay before noting what helps.
- **Your wording.** A script can be direct, brief, tentative or silent, according to your preference.
- **Specific enough to act on.** "Please write the next step" tells a reader what you are requesting.
- **Disclosure is a choice.** Keep private detail out of an extract unless you want it included.
- **Changing needs are allowed.** A useful request in one setting may not fit another.
- **The card is a support, not a test.** Forgetting it, choosing not to show it or having a request refused is not a failure of self-advocacy.

## 5. Core components

These are the existing profile fields; this guide adds no new required data.

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `need` | What would make the situation more accessible. Required and repeatable |
| `helps` | A support or adjustment; repeatable |
| `context` | Where or when the need applies |
| `script` | Words to copy, read aloud, show or put into a communication tool |
| `share-with` | A note to yourself about an intended reader |
| `body` | Optional longer detail |

**`share-with` is not access control.** It does not encrypt, hide, redact or restrict a file. Anyone who receives the full file can read its other entries. Select the intended text before sharing and inspect the result.

## 6. Setting it up

1. Choose one recurring situation, such as an appointment, a meeting or a busy shop.
2. Write the need in ordinary words: "time to read before answering".
3. Add one thing that might help: "send the question in writing".
4. Write a short request you would feel comfortable showing.
5. Decide where you can retrieve it. A small paper card may be easier than finding a phone file.
6. If you make a shareable copy, remove detail the reader does not need and check the copy itself.

You can stop after step 2. Testing a script with someone you trust is optional, and you need not disclose the collection to do so.

## 7. Daily practice

There is no daily page. Before a situation, retrieve one relevant phrase if you want it. During it, you can speak, show, point, use AAC or ask a chosen person to read the phrase.

Afterward, an optional note can say "use written questions again" or "this wording did not fit". There is no need to rate how well you advocated for yourself. A refusal is information about the situation, not proof that the need was unreasonable.

## 8. Rhythms

- **Before an appointment or meeting:** choose one relevant request and an accessible way to present it.
- **Weekly, only if useful:** put a frequently used phrase somewhere easier to reach.
- **Monthly, optional:** remove outdated contact details or wording you no longer want to share.
- **After a long gap:** use the collection as it is or start a new card. No catch-up is needed.

An old script can remain useful; a review date is not an expiration date on a need.

## 9. Page anatomy

```text
For: busy reception desk

Need: time to read before answering
Helps: the question written down
Words I can show:
"Please write the question. I need a little time to read it."

Private note: I only want to show the request above.
```

Keep the private note on another side or another page if the whole card will be handed over. A fold or cover is a practical reminder, not a security guarantee.

## 10. Worked examples

All examples are invented. They illustrate recording and sharing choices, not guaranteed responses.

**A day:** a writer chooses this plain-text entry before a meeting:

```jot
:::record collection
method: access-needs-and-scripts@1
need: Time to read before responding
helps: Questions in writing
helps: A pause before the discussion moves on
context: Team meetings
script: Please send the question in the chat and give me time to read it.
share-with: Meeting chair, if I choose
:::
```

They copy the `script` sentence into the meeting chat. The stored `share-with` note did not restrict the source file; they chose the sentence separately.

**A week:** the writer notices that the same request also fits a phone appointment. They add a separate entry asking for a written summary. They do not duplicate private meeting notes into the appointment extract.

**A month:** the writer prefers "Please give me a moment" to a longer explanation. They replace one script, keep another and remove an old shared copy from the place they control. They cannot assume that a recipient deleted an earlier copy.

**An overloaded moment:** a card says "I need a quieter place. Please ask one question at a time." The writer points to it and writes nothing afterward. That is a complete use of the collection.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

- **Need-only list:** keep the required `need` with no script or history.
- **Pocket card:** one request and no identifying detail.
- **Appointment note:** a short chosen extract for a particular clinician or receptionist.
- **AAC phrase bank:** wording selected through the writer's usual communication system.
- **Trusted-person script:** "Please read this request for me, without adding the private notes."
- **Home reference:** information about where a support is stored, without a plan to share it.

These are proposed adaptations, not separately evaluated methods.

## 12. Community practice

People use access cards, written communication, accommodation letters and personal instructions in many disability communities. This guide does not establish their prevalence or a single preferred term.

A public template should use invented examples. Someone else's disclosed needs, contact details or private scripts do not become reusable sample content without their permission.

## 13. Official products and formats

There is no official product for this Jot profile and no card purchase is required. A paid identification or access-card scheme is a separate service with its own rules. This collection neither endorses a scheme nor establishes formal eligibility.

## 14. Tools and supplies

Use a card, a notebook, a locally saved file or an existing communication aid. Large text, a durable cover or a duplicate card can help retrieval if you want them. A short handwritten phrase is enough.

If a phone is unreliable, keep a paper alternative. If paper is hard to handle or read, use your existing accessible device. Do not make buying a tool a condition of asking for support.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

Plain text can be searched, enlarged, read aloud or copied into another communication tool. Paper can be available without a charged device. Neither medium guarantees privacy.

A shared screen, a cloud backup, a lock-screen preview or a found notebook may reveal more than intended. Keep the private collection separate from a prepared extract when that helps. Check exported text, filenames and surrounding entries before sending or handing it over.

This profile does not automatically send requests, grant permissions or notify the people named in `share-with`.

## 16. Evidence and research

**Level: anecdotal for the collection itself.** No cited source tests this field set, reusable-script journal or its effect on access, wellbeing or camouflaging. This is a focused source review, not a systematic literature search.

| Source | What it supports | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NICE CG142, especially recommendations 1.1.4, 1.1.5 and 1.1.8 [1] | Accessible information, attention to communication needs, communication aids and individualized environmental adjustments | Clinical guidance for adult health and social care. It does not validate this journal, apply identically in every setting or guarantee a response |
| Hull et al., 2017 [2] | Qualitative accounts from 92 autistic adults of camouflaging and its consequences | Does not test scripts, establish that every person wants to disclose needs or prove that a card reduces exhaustion |
| W3C explanation of WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.3.2 [3] | Clear labels and instructions so a person knows what an input asks for | Web accessibility guidance, not a trial of paper cards; following one criterion does not establish full accessibility |
| EEOC reasonable-accommodation guidance [4] | US employment guidance permits requests in plain English without requiring the phrase "reasonable accommodation" | Specific legal context with conditions, documentation and undue-hardship rules. It is not a promise of eligibility, universal law or advice for every request |

The step from these sources to a personal collection is a design inference: keeping wording available may be useful to a writer. Whether it reduces effort, improves access or feels safe needs evaluation with willing writers, including people using different communication methods.

## 17. Benefits

Intended uses, not demonstrated outcomes of this method:

- Keep a phrase available without composing it during a difficult moment.
- Separate private context from the request you choose to share.
- Remember what support you want in a particular setting.
- Change a request without rewriting an entire personal history.

A need can be valid even when the collection is unused or a request is declined. The guide provides no measure of advocacy skill or success.

## 18. Pitfalls

- **Treating a field as a lock:** `share-with` records an intention only. Review the actual shared copy.
- **Disclosing too much:** a short request may be enough for an informal situation. Formal processes can ask for additional information; seek relevant local guidance rather than assuming one script fits them all.
- **Scripts become demands on the writer:** no one must read, show or rehearse a card to deserve support.
- **A helpful person speaks over you:** agree what they may communicate and whether they may add context.
- **Assuming one request always fits:** change the request with the setting, reader or preference.
- **Keeping unsafe detail:** omit names, diagnoses or explanations if recording them would create unwanted exposure.

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **Overload or low capacity:** one request, a familiar symbol with text, pointing or no saved entry can be enough.
- **Non-speaking or intermittent speech:** use AAC, a typed sentence, a card or a chosen reader. Speech is not required to make the collection yours.
- **Vision:** large text, high contrast, a screen-reader-friendly list and written labels beside symbols. Do not use color as the only signal.
- **Motor access:** keep phrases in an existing switch-access or voice-control tool, or ask for help preparing a card in your words.
- **Processing and memory:** use concrete wording and keep one request per card. Optional headings should not become a form you must complete [3].
- **Language:** prepare a translation with a person or service you trust if you want one; check that it keeps your meaning. NICE specifically discusses communication aids and independent interpreters in care [1].
- **Privacy:** an unlabelled card or a request without diagnostic detail may suit you better. Choose whether and when it is shown.

## 21. Combining

A [stim menu](stimming-and-self-regulation.md) can hold preferred supports; this collection can hold the words for asking that one be available. A [task-transition card](task-initiation-and-transitions.md) can include retrieving a prepared request as its next step.

Do not turn the combination into extra required work. A [low-demand check-in](low-demand-check-in.md) can remain separate or be omitted entirely. No cross-reference gives another person permission to read either record.

## 22. Ready-to-use bank

Original examples to change, ignore or copy:

- "Please write the next step down."
- "I need a moment to read before answering."
- "Please ask one question at a time."
- "Could we move somewhere quieter?"
- "Please tell me before changing the plan."
- "I would like to use my communication device."
- "Please address the question to me."
- "I can share the request, but I do not want to share my private notes."
- "Please give me a written summary of what we agreed."
- "This support does not fit today. I would like to try another option."

No example guarantees acceptance or replaces a formal process.

## 23. Glossary

- **AAC:** augmentative and alternative communication; tools and methods that support or replace speech.
- **Access need:** something that affects whether a setting or activity is usable for the person.
- **Accommodation or adjustment:** a change requested or made to support access; legal meanings vary.
- **Extract:** only the selected text prepared for a reader.
- **Script:** words kept for possible reuse, not a requirement to speak them exactly.
- **`share-with`:** the writer's note about an intended reader, not a permission setting.

## 24. FAQ

**Do I need a diagnosis?** No diagnosis is required to keep the collection. A particular formal accommodation process may have its own requirements.

**Must I explain why?** The collection does not require it. What information a formal process can request depends on its rules and jurisdiction [4].

**Can someone help me write it?** Yes, if you choose. Keep your meaning and agree which parts they may see or share.

**Does `share-with: clinician` hide other entries?** No. Prepare and check a separate extract.

**What if I cannot show the card?** The card has no attendance requirement. You can use another route, ask for help or leave it unused.

## 25. For Journalwright Studio

This heading preserves the shared guide structure. For Jot, these are design recommendations, not claims about measured effects or new features.

Adopt a need-only starting point, optional reusable wording, a plain-language explanation of `share-with`, and a preview of any chosen extract before sharing. Preserve words that express uncertainty or a changed preference.

Do not infer a diagnosis, turn advocacy into a score, share the full collection by default or imply that a request guarantees a service. Never present the text of `share-with` as an enforced access rule.

## 26. Open questions

- Which formats are easiest to retrieve during overload, including for people with substantial communication or motor support needs?
- Does a collection reduce the effort of asking, or does preparing it add another demand?
- What do writers consider safe to store, and how does that change across home, school, employment and healthcare settings?
- How can a chosen helper support the request without taking over the writer's decisions?

These questions need input from willing writers; the current guide does not answer them through outcome studies.
