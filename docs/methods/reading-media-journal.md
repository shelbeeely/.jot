# Reading and media journals

A reading or media journal connects a work to the writer's response: what stayed with them, what they question, or what they want to revisit. Jot's `reading-media-journal@1` covers books, films, games and other works without requiring ratings or completion [1].

Sources checked 2026-11-02; research abstracts rather than full papers were reviewed, as recorded in section 28. Evidence for specific learning techniques does not establish benefits for every kind of media journal.

## 2. Summary

- Record the work and your response; either can be brief.
- Distinguish a quotation, a factual summary and your interpretation.
- Add an edition, page, chapter, timestamp or other locator when it helps.
- Mark spoilers before sharing, and inspect the actual shared excerpt.
- Leaving a work unfinished is a valid experience to record.

## 3. History and origin

This is a broad practice rather than a single branded method. Commonplace books, study notes, reviews and fan discussions overlap with it, but have different aims.

| Date | Relevant research or definition |
| --- | --- |
| 2006 | Roediger and Karpicke test retrieval versus repeated study of prose passages [2] |
| 2013 | Dunlosky and colleagues review ten learning techniques [3] |
| 2016 | Panero and colleagues report a replication attempt on reading literary fiction and immediate theory-of-mind performance [4] |
| 2026 | Jot defines a response-focused profile that does not require reading progress or a rating [1] |

These dates describe the sources reviewed here, not the invention of responding to books or other works.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- A response can be personal without claiming to speak for all readers.
- The work and the writer's reaction remain distinct.
- An exact quotation needs checking; a paraphrase needs to be labelled as your wording.
- Pleasure, boredom, uncertainty and stopping are all recordable.
- Learning goals are optional; leisure does not need to become an assignment.

The profile preserves a response. It does not decide what the work means or whether the writer has interpreted it correctly.

## 5. Core components

Jot uses a `reflection` record [1].

| Field | Role |
| --- | --- |
| `work` | Required: the work being discussed |
| `body` | Required: the writer's response |
| `creator` | Optional creator name or credit |
| `locator` | Optional place within the work; repeatable |
| `quotation` | Optional attributed words copied accurately |
| `spoiler` | Optional `true` or `false` |
| `source` | Optional source information; repeatable |

An identifying title can be enough at first. Avoid guessing a creator or publication detail; add it later after checking.

## 6. Setting it up

1. Decide the purpose: remember a reaction, gather questions, prepare discussion or study something.
2. Choose a small space beside or separate from the work.
3. Write the title and one thing that stayed with you.
4. Add a locator only if you may want to find that part again.
5. If sharing, decide the audience and whether the note reveals plot details.

During the first week, notice which fields you actually use. Remove a rating, progress chart or quotation box if it creates work without helping your purpose.

## 7. Daily practice

No daily entry is required. A useful stopping-point note can be as small as "chapter 3: I don't yet trust this narrator."

For a film or game, you might record a scene, sound, choice or accessibility experience after the session. A response to an audiobook is equally a reading response; no transcription quota is necessary.

If learning is the goal, try recalling a point before reopening the source, then check and correct it. This uses a related learning technique [2, 3]; it is not required for enjoyment or reflection.

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | Optional use |
| --- | --- |
| During a work | Keep one question or locator without breaking the experience repeatedly |
| At a stopping point | Write a reaction while distinguishing memory from a checked quotation |
| Weekly | Revisit one question, or choose something else without completing the current work |
| Monthly | Look for interests worth following, without ranking reading volume |

A month without an entry needs no reconstruction. If writing from memory later, say so when that affects the accuracy of a summary or quotation.

## 9. Page anatomy

```text
Work / creator / version, if useful
Where: chapter, page, scene, timestamp, level or episode

My response:
Question to keep:

Optional exact quotation, clearly separated from my words
Sharing note: spoilers? audience?
```

A page can hold just the work and the response. The other lines are options, not a completeness test.

## 10. Worked examples

All works, creators, quotations and situations in these examples are invented.

**A day:** a reader wants to keep a reaction without revealing an ending.

```jot
:::record reflection #quiet-scene
method: reading-media-journal@1
work: The Glass Orchard (invented novel)
body: The quiet scene in the kitchen stayed with me more than the argument.
spoiler: false
:::
```

**A week:** they return to the scene and add a separate, checked note. Here the source and quotation are fictional example data.

```jot
:::record reflection #door-detail
method: reading-media-journal@1
work: The Glass Orchard (invented novel)
creator: Example Author
locator: Chapter 4
quotation: The door made no sound.
source: Invented source for this worked example
body: I read this as a pause rather than a resolution. That is my interpretation.
spoiler: true
:::
```

**A month:** a viewer compares the silence in the novel with sound design in an invented film. They link the two in their notes without claiming that the creators intended the same effect. A different reader's interpretation can sit alongside theirs.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variation | Emphasis |
| --- | --- |
| Reading diary | How an encounter with a work felt at a particular time |
| Quotation notebook | Attributable passages and locators |
| Film or music response | Scenes, sound, performance and personal associations |
| Game journal | Choices, experiences and accessibility, not mandatory achievement tracking |
| Discussion preparation | Questions and passages to revisit with an agreed group |
| Study companion | Retrieval questions and later checking [2, 3] |

Keep factual checking separate from deciding whether a work mattered to you.

## 12. Community practice

Book groups, review communities and fan discussions use different conventions for spoilers, attribution and disclosure. Ask about the particular group's expectations rather than assuming one universal practice.

Public ratings and reading totals are social formats, not requirements of this method. A private response can be tentative, contradictory or too personal to publish.

## 13. Official products and formats

There is no official notebook or required app. Jot's record shape is defined in its profile catalogue [1]. A library book, freely available work or familiar work revisited from memory can be a starting point.

No commercial product prices are listed because none is necessary. This guide does not evaluate the privacy policies of third-party reading or media platforms.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- A bookmark or quick note can hold a locator until you want to respond.
- A notebook or plain-text file can keep your own words separately from platform reviews.
- Voice input may reduce writing effort; inspect any transcript you intend to quote.
- A text search or index can find repeated themes without turning them into scores.
- An edition or version note can explain why page numbers differ.

Do not assume that saving a passage grants permission to republish the whole work.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

Digital notes can search titles and locators; paper can remain beside a book without switching apps. Neither medium has established superiority for this general practice.

A timestamp may locate a film scene, while an e-book locator may depend on the edition or platform. Include enough context to recognise the passage if the original link stops working.

Before sharing, inspect quotations, spoilers and personal associations. A `spoiler` field describes the note; it does not enforce hiding or selective access. Private reflections should not be mixed into a publicly shared file.

## 16. Evidence and research

Evidence level: **some for related components**, not a demonstrated effect of `reading-media-journal@1`.

| Source | Finding in scope | Limit for this guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roediger and Karpicke 2006 [2] | In two experiments with prose passages, retrieval practice improved delayed retention compared with repeated study | Student learning tasks; not evidence that any reaction, rating or quotation log improves memory |
| Dunlosky et al. 2013 [3] | Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility ratings; summarisation and rereading had more limited generality | A learning-technique review, not a comparison of media journals or reading for pleasure |
| Panero et al. 2016 [4] | A three-team replication effort with 792 participants across four conditions found no immediate theory-of-mind advantage from literary fiction versus comparison conditions | It does not settle all long-term effects of reading, and did not test a journal |

Copying a passage is not automatically retrieval practice. Writing a personal reaction is not automatically an accurate summary. These distinctions matter when choosing a learning activity rather than a reflective one.

Claims that this journal reliably increases empathy, intelligence, reading volume or wellbeing are not established by the checked sources.

## 17. Benefits

- It keeps a response connected to a work: a record property.
- Locators make passages easier to find again: a practical affordance.
- Distinguishing quotation and interpretation makes the provenance of a note inspectable: a design choice.
- Optional retrieval questions can apply a supported learning technique [2, 3], but their effect depends on the task and how they are used.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Difficulty | Possible adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| The journal interrupts every page | Keep one marker and respond later |
| Reading becomes a completion contest | Remove totals or progress fields |
| A remembered line becomes an exact quotation | Check it, or label it as a paraphrase |
| A response is mistaken for fact | Write "I read this as..." and separate checked details |
| Spoilers travel with a supposedly safe excerpt | Inspect the full shared copy, including headings and attachments |
| Notes become longer than wanted | Keep one sentence or stop taking notes |

Not liking or finishing a work does not need a justification.

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **Vision:** keep text notes that can be enlarged or read aloud.
- **Motor effort:** use a short voice note through a chosen accessible workflow, then review any transcript.
- **Cognitive load:** keep title plus one reaction; add bibliographic detail only when needed.
- **Dyslexia or reading fatigue:** include audiobooks, performances and adapted editions without treating them as lesser experiences.
- **Sensory load:** note volume, captions or a needed pause if useful to you.
- **Memory:** use a scene description as well as a number when locating a passage is difficult.

These are access choices. They do not imply that the journal remedies an access barrier in the original work.

## 21. Combining

Use a commonplace entry for a quotation and a reading response for what it meant to you. A study page can hold a question to retrieve later without converting every leisure response into revision.

For a [shared journal](shared-journal.md), choose the exact response to share and agree the audience. A book-group invitation does not imply permission to publish someone else's notes.

## 22. Ready-to-use bank

- What stayed with me?
- I read this scene as ___; another reading might be ___ .
- A question I want to keep is ___ .
- Exact quotation, checked against ___ at ___:
- My paraphrase, not the work's wording:
- Before looking again, I remember ___; after checking, ___ .
- I stopped here because ___, if I want to record why.
- This note contains spoilers for ___ .

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning here |
| --- | --- |
| Locator | A place within a work, such as chapter, page or timestamp |
| Paraphrase | Another person's idea expressed in your own words |
| Quotation | Exact words attributed to their source |
| Response | The writer's reaction or interpretation |
| Spoiler | Detail that reveals an experience another person may prefer to encounter first-hand |

## 24. FAQ

**Must I finish the work first?** No. A response can be written at any point.

**Do I need a star rating?** No. Ratings and progress are not required fields [1].

**Can I record a podcast or game?** Yes. Name the work and use a useful locator if wanted.

**Will writing notes help me remember?** Specific learning activities have evidence [2, 3]; merely keeping this journal has not been shown to guarantee retention.

**Does reading fiction make everyone more empathetic?** The checked replication does not support that simple immediate-effect claim [4].

**Is `spoiler: false` a privacy setting?** No. Review the text before sharing it.

## 25. For Jot

Require only `work` and `body` in a structured record. Preserve optional creator, quotation, locator and source details, including repeatable `source` and `locator` fields; keep `spoiler` boolean [1].

Keep response distinct from a generated or verified summary. Do not invent quotations or publication details, require reading progress, rank the writer's taste or infer a psychological trait from their media choices. Sharing and external metadata lookup are separate explicit actions.
