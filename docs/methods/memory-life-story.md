# Memory, grief and life-story writing

Memory writing keeps a recollection and what it means to the writer, without treating remembering as proof. Jot's `memory-life-story@1` permits uncertain details and several accounts of the same event [1].

> Health note: writing about loss is optional. This guide is not a grief treatment, memory test or way to recover hidden events. If grief is making daily life difficult, consider support from an appropriate health professional or bereavement service [4].

Sources checked 2026-11-02, with access details in section 28. Clinical reminiscence interventions, oral-history ethics and memory research inform separate parts of this guide; they do not validate the exact Jot format.

## 2. Summary

- Write what you remember and what you do not know.
- A sensory fragment can stand without a complete scene.
- Keep remembered detail separate from evidence checked later.
- Let different people retain different accounts.
- Choose whether and when to revisit, preserve, remove or share the writing.

## 3. History and origin

Life stories, memoirs, family recollections and oral histories have different traditions and purposes. This guide does not assign them one inventor or require one cultural model of remembrance.

| Date | Relevant source |
| --- | --- |
| 2007 | Schacter and Addis review episodic memory as a constructive process, not a literal replay [2] |
| 2018 | Woods and colleagues update a Cochrane review of reminiscence therapy for people with dementia [3] |
| 2018 | OHA revises ethical guidance on recording, reviewing and releasing oral histories [5] |
| 2026 | Jot defines a memory reflection profile that explicitly preserves uncertainty [1] |

Clinical reminiscence and private remembrance overlap in using past experiences, but are not interchangeable interventions.

## 4. Philosophy and principles

- **Remembering is not verification.** A vivid recollection can still have uncertain details [2].
- **A fragment is enough.** The writer need not supply an ending, lesson or complete chronology.
- **Uncertainty belongs in the record.** "I think" and "I do not know" should survive editing.
- **Other accounts can coexist.** A shared history does not require merging voices.
- **Revisiting is chosen.** An anniversary is not permission for an app to surface a difficult entry.
- **Sharing has an audience.** Recording a story and authorising its release are separate choices [5].

## 5. Core components

The Jot profile uses record type `reflection` [1].

| Field | Role |
| --- | --- |
| `body` | Required: the memory or reflection |
| `person` | Optional person connected to the memory; avoid identifying detail you do not need |
| `period` | Optional time period, including uncertainty |
| `confidence` | Optional words describing what is clear or doubtful; not a factual accuracy score |
| `asset` | Optional associated photograph, recording or other asset |

An asset can prompt a recollection without verifying every part of it. A photograph's date may establish when that photograph was made, while the associated story still needs its own uncertainty note.

## 6. Setting it up

1. Decide whether this is for you, selected people or a possible future archive.
2. Choose one small memory, object or place you are willing to approach.
3. Write what comes to mind without filling gaps to make a complete scene.
4. Add what you are uncertain about, if it matters.
5. Stop or change the subject when you want to.

If recording another person's story, agree purpose, access, recording and possible reuse before starting. Give them a chance to review the account before release [5]. Do not promise confidentiality or deletion that you cannot deliver.

## 7. Daily practice

There is no daily requirement. A brief session might hold one sound, a remembered phrase or a place description.

Keep a gentle stopping point available: "That is enough for this entry." You can write something ordinary afterward, put the page away or leave it unfinished.

For a grief entry, there is no need to produce gratitude, forgiveness or a statement of closure. NHS guidance recognises varied responses to loss; there is no right or wrong way to feel [4].

## 8. Rhythms

| Rhythm | Optional use |
| --- | --- |
| One session | Keep one memory without completing a life chapter |
| Weekly | Add a detail, a different account or nothing |
| Monthly | Check whether the storage and audience choices still fit |
| Anniversary or milestone | Decide whether to revisit; declining is a complete choice |

After a gap, resume with any period you want. Do not infer the order of events from the order in which memories were written.

## 9. Page anatomy

```text
Memory or working title:
What I remember:

Time period, if known:
What I am unsure about:
Checked source, if any, kept distinct from recollection:

Optional audience or later-note agreement, outside the memory itself
```

These are suggested headings. Only the body is required for the structured profile; no feeling or interpretation needs to be supplied [1].

## 10. Worked examples

All people, memories and situations below are invented.

**A day:** a writer remembers a sound more clearly than a date.

```jot
:::record reflection #radio-memory
method: memory-life-story@1
body: I remember a small radio on the windowsill and someone humming while washing cups.
person: A relative
period: One summer during childhood; year uncertain
confidence: The sound is clear to me; I am unsure which house this was.
:::
```

**A week:** someone else remembers the radio in a different room. The writer keeps both accounts, without deciding that repetition makes either one verified.

```jot
:::record reflection #radio-other-account
method: memory-life-story@1
body: A relative remembers the radio in the hallway. This is their account, recorded with permission; I still picture the windowsill.
confidence: We have not checked a photograph or dated record.
:::
```

**A month:** the writer finds a photograph showing a radio near a window. They add a labelled later note describing what the photograph actually shows. They do not infer who was humming, the exact date or the conversation from that image alone.

## 11. Variations and offshoots

| Variation | Emphasis and boundary |
| --- | --- |
| Life-story fragments | Places or moments without a complete chronology |
| Family-history notes | Distinguish documents, remembered accounts and unanswered questions |
| Remembrance page | An aspect of a person, not an obligation to summarise their whole life |
| Object-led writing | Begin with a familiar object; leave ownership or dates uncertain if necessary |
| Oral-history project | Additional recording, consent, review and release arrangements [5] |
| Clinical reminiscence | A defined facilitated intervention in a particular care setting [3] |

Do not rename private journaling "reminiscence therapy" merely because it uses an old photograph.

## 12. Community practice

Archives, family-history projects, memoir groups and bereavement settings have different expectations. Ask what will happen to a contribution before offering a personal story.

OHA guidance treats the narrator as a participant with choices about recording and release, while acknowledging power differences and the limits of promises about later use [5]. This offers an ethical model, not a universal rule about how families must remember.

## 13. Official products and formats

There is no required official memory book or subscription. The local Jot profile defines the optional structured fields [1].

Commercial prompt books may offer convenient pages, but they are not evidence that completing every question is helpful. No prices are included because no product is needed for this practice.

## 14. Tools and supplies

- A notebook or plain-text file can hold a fragment.
- A photograph, object or music title may provide a starting point; using a prompt is optional.
- Keep the original identifier of a source if you may need to find it again.
- A chosen recording or transcription tool can support access, with permission from anyone recorded.
- Preserve an original and label a transcript or edited version as such.

An attractive album is not necessary. Protecting and finding a record may matter more than completing its presentation.

## 15. Digital and hybrid versions

Digital text allows search and enlargement. Paper may feel simpler to put away. Both can be discovered by other people, so choose identifying details and storage with that possibility in mind.

Assets can reveal faces, voices, locations, dates and other people's information. Review the whole shared copy, not just the prose. A plain `.jot` file does not enforce audience restrictions.

OCR, transcription and generated text can introduce errors. Review any transformed text; never insert a generated missing detail as remembered fact [1]. Automatic anniversary resurfacing should not be assumed to be welcome.

## 16. Evidence and research

Evidence level: **some for related interventions and memory processes**, not an efficacy finding for `memory-life-story@1`.

| Source | What it supports | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schacter and Addis 2007 [2] | Episodic memory is constructive and can be prone to errors and illusions | That vividness, confidence or a writing prompt verifies an event |
| Woods et al. 2018 [3] | Some outcomes of facilitated reminiscence interventions for people with dementia differed by setting and modality | That private life-story or grief writing prevents dementia, restores factual memory or has equivalent effects |
| NHS grief guidance [4] | Different feelings after loss; available support when coping is difficult | A trial of grief journaling or an ideal writing schedule |
| OHA ethics [5] | Informed consent, review and authorisation in oral-history work | Clinical efficacy of memory writing |

The Cochrane review included 22 studies involving 1,972 people with dementia. Overall quality-of-life effects were not important immediately after intervention; some subgroups and outcomes showed small benefits. Results were inconsistent, and carers' outcomes did not establish a general benefit [3].

These findings should not become a promise that making a memory book improves cognition or resolves grief. No exact-format efficacy trial was identified in this targeted source review.

## 17. Benefits

- A memory can be preserved alongside its uncertainties: a record property.
- Separate accounts can remain visible: a design choice.
- Checked sources can be attached without erasing the original recollection: an archival affordance.
- Some facilitated reminiscence interventions have bounded clinical evidence [3]; private writing should not inherit those results.

Personal meaning is something the writer determines, not an outcome an application can guarantee.

## 18. Pitfalls

| Difficulty | Possible response |
| --- | --- |
| Filling gaps to make a convincing story | Leave them visible or label imagined passages as fiction |
| Treating a confident memory as proof | Keep the confidence note separate from source verification |
| Leading someone toward a suggested event | Ask open questions and allow "I don't remember" |
| Feeling required to finish a grief narrative | Stop; no closure statement is required |
| Family disagreement | Preserve attribution and different accounts without forcing a verdict |
| Unexpected resurfacing | Keep difficult material out of automatic reminders or public displays |

If writing repeatedly becomes overwhelming or disrupts daily life, pause and consider appropriate support [4]. This is not a requirement to revisit distressing events.

## 19. Accessibility and adaptations

- **Memory differences:** accept fragments, approximate periods and "I don't know" without correction drills.
- **Vision:** use large text or described images; avoid an image-only album when text access matters.
- **Motor effort:** dictate through a chosen workflow or work with a trusted scribe.
- **Communication:** allow pointing, a short phrase or another chosen means of expression.
- **Energy:** one detail can finish a session; organising the whole archive is optional.
- **Supported writing:** distinguish the narrator's words from a helper's interpretation, and agree the helper's access [5].

Do not test a person on dates or identities in order to permit their contribution.

## 21. Combining

A [shared journal](shared-journal.md) can hold separate recollections with consent. A commonplace entry can hold a checked quotation from a letter, while the memory reflection says what that letter brings to mind.

A free diary paragraph can remain unstructured. There is no need to convert every mention of a past event into a formal memory record or family-history claim.

## 22. Ready-to-use bank

- A small detail I remember is ___ .
- I am unsure whether ___ .
- This part comes from a photograph or document, rather than my own memory: ___ .
- Another person remembers it this way, shared with permission: ___ .
- Later note, added on ___ after checking ___:
- A question I want to leave unanswered is ___ .
- I want this kept for ___, if anyone.
- I do not want to revisit or share this now.

Avoid prompts that imply a hidden event must have happened or that a complete account can be recovered by writing harder.

## 23. Glossary

| Term | Meaning here |
| --- | --- |
| Account | One person's description, which may differ from another's |
| Confidence | How clear or doubtful something feels, not proof of accuracy |
| Life story | Selected recollections and interpretations, not an exhaustive record |
| Provenance | Where an account or source came from |
| Reminiscence | Remembering and talking or writing about past experiences |
| Verification | Checking a factual claim against relevant independent evidence |

## 24. FAQ

**What if I cannot remember the date?** Use an approximate period or omit it. The body is the only required field [1].

**Should different relatives agree on one version?** They can keep separate attributed accounts. Agreement is not necessary to preserve a recollection.

**Will writing recover forgotten events?** This guide makes no such claim. Do not treat a prompted or generated scene as verified memory [2].

**Must a remembrance be positive?** No. You can keep mixed feelings, choose a neutral detail or choose not to write.

**Is a memory book dementia treatment?** No. Evidence for defined reminiscence interventions does not establish that claim [3].

**Does grief have a writing deadline?** No schedule is established here. Seek support if coping is difficult rather than requiring the diary to solve it [4].

## 25. For Jot

Require only `body`. Keep optional `person`, `period`, `confidence` and `asset`; do not turn confidence into a clinical or factual score [1]. Preserve several accounts and later corrections without silently rewriting the earlier words.

Never generate missing details as remembered facts. Resurfacing and sharing are explicit choices. A future interface should allow a writer to leave a memory closed, and should distinguish original writing from transcriptions, helper contributions and checked external sources.
